Non-Dual Shaiva Tantra (specifically the Kashmir Shaivism of Abhinavagupta) is characterized by a specific blend of monistic metaphysics (everything is Consciousness/Shiva), world-affirmation (the world is a real play/expression of the Divine, not an illusion), and soteriology of recognition (liberation is recognizing one's own nature).

While there is no single Western tradition that maps 1:1 onto this specific matrix—largely because Western mysticism often struggled against dualistic orthodoxies (Platonism’s separation of matter/spirit or Christian orthodoxy’s Creator/creature distinction)—there are powerful parallels in specific currents.

Here are the closest Western equivalents categorized by their resonance with Tantra.

1. Neoplatonic Theurgy (The "Ritual" Parallel)
While standard Neoplatonism (Plotinus) is often ascetic and world-denying, the later Iamblichean Neoplatonism creates a strong bridge to Tantra.

Theurgy (God-Working): Unlike Plotinus, who advocated solitary contemplation, Iamblichus argued that the human soul is too deeply embodied to ascend via intellect alone. He introduced theurgy—rituals involving matter, statues, and invocations—to unite the soul with the divine. This mirrors the Tantric use of ritual (puja, mantra) as a necessary vehicle for realization.

Sacramental Universe: For the theurgists, the material world was filled with "synthemata" (divine tokens or symbols) hidden inside matter by the Demiurge. Engaging with these symbols allows the practitioner to resonate with the divine, similar to the Tantric view of the world as the vibrant energy (Shakti) of the divine.

2. Renaissance Hermeticism & Alchemy (The "Transmutation" Parallel)
Hermeticism offers a "Western Tantra" in its view of the relationship between the macrocosm and microcosm.

"As Above, So Below": This axiom mirrors the Tantric view of the body as a fractal of the cosmos.

Alchemy as Spiritual Technology: Much like Hatha Yoga (which emerged from Tantric contexts), Alchemy views the material world and the body not as obstacles to be escaped, but as dense spirit to be refined and transmuted. The goal is not to leave matter, but to perfect it (the Philosopher's Stone/Diamond Body).

The Imagination: Renaissance magicians (like Giordano Bruno or Marsilio Ficino) utilized the active imagination to manipulate reality and consciousness, closely parallel to Tantric visualization practices (bhavana).

3. German Idealism & Nature Philosophy (The "Philosophical" Parallel)
If you are looking for the intellectual rigor of Abhinavagupta without the ritual, F.W.J. Schelling is perhaps the closest Western philosopher to a Tantric non-dualist.

Naturphilosophie: Schelling argued that Nature is "visible Spirit" and Spirit is "invisible Nature." He rejected the dead, mechanical view of the universe in favor of a dynamic, self-organizing organic whole.

The Absolute: His view of the Absolute as an indifferent ground that splits itself into subject and object to know itself is strikingly similar to the concept of Shiva limiting himself to become the finite jiva (individual soul) for the joy of rediscovering his own nature (Lila).

4. Radical Christian Mysticism (The "Non-Dual" Parallel)
While mainstream Christianity maintains a distinction between Creator and Creation, the Rhineland Mystics break this boundary.

Meister Eckhart: Eckhart distinguishes between "God" (the persona) and the "Godhead" (the absolute ground). He famously prayed "to God to rid me of God," seeking the pre-dual state. His concept of the "Ground" (Grund) where the soul’s ground and God’s ground are the same is a pure non-dual realization.

The Difference: Eckhart is generally more apophatic (negative theology/emptiness) and less body-positive than Shaiva Tantra. He leans closer to Advaita Vedanta (illusionism) than the vibrant, world-embracing non-duality of Tantra.

5. Phenomenology of the Will (The "Shakti" Parallel)
In the 19th century, Arthur Schopenhauer and Friedrich Nietzsche inverted the Western preference for "Intellect" and placed "Will" at the center.

Will to Power: Nietzsche’s affirmation of the chaotic, creative, destructive force of life—and the desire to say "Yes" to it (Amor Fati)—has a distinct "Shaktic" flavor. It embraces the totality of existence, including the terrible, rather than seeking a sterile heaven.

Metaphysics	Everything is Shiva/Consciousness	German Idealism (Hegel/Schelling)
Methodology	Ritual, Mantra, Embodiment	Theurgy (Iamblichus) & Hermeticism
View of Matter	Immanent Divinity (Shakti)	Alchemy (Hidden gold in lead)

The "Missing Link"
The element most unique to Tantra that is hardest to find in the West is the somatic technology—the detailed mapping of the subtle body (chakras, nadis) combined with non-dual philosophy. While the West has gymnastics (physical) and prayer (spiritual), the integration of the two into a soteriological system of "divinizing the body" is less developed, appearing mostly in fragmented forms in Alchemy and ritual magic.

If you are interested in a modern synthesis, the work of Carl Jung often serves as a Western bridge, as he attempted to recover the Alchemical/Hermetic tradition and interpret it through a psychological lens that accommodates both the light and dark aspects of the self.

Theurgy (from the Greek theoi "gods" + ergon "work") is the practice of "god-working."

While theology is "talking about God," theurgy is the act of uniting with the Divine through ritual action.

It emerged most prominently in late Neoplatonism (3rd–4th century CE), specifically through the philosopher Iamblichus, who argued that the human intellect alone was insufficient for salvation. This is the closest Western equivalent to the Sadhana (spiritual practice) found in Tantra, because it relies on the idea that matter is not an obstacle to spirit, but a vehicle for it.

The Core Conflict: Plotinus vs. Iamblichus
To understand theurgy, you have to understand the argument that birthed it.

Plotinus (The Purist): Believed the soul could return to the One simply through philosophical contemplation (theoria). He viewed the body and matter as a "snare" or a distraction.

Iamblichus (The Theurgist): Argued that the soul is deeply enmeshed in matter. You cannot simply "think" your way out of the body because your mind is part of the trap. You need an external lever—a ritual mechanism—to lift the soul up.

The Mechanics of Theurgy
Theurgy operates on a specific metaphysical technology that is strikingly similar to the Tantric view of mantra and yantra.

1. Synthemata (Divine Tokens)
This is the central concept. Iamblichus taught that the Demiurge (Creator) sowed "signatures" or "tokens" (synthemata) of the divine throughout the material world.

These tokens exist in stones, plants, animals, scents, and specific sounds.

A theurgist doesn’t create a connection to the gods; they activate a connection that already exists.

Example: If a specific god is associated with the sun, the theurgist utilizes gold (metal), heliotrope (plant), and specific solar invocations. These are not just symbols; they are "live wires" of that god's energy embedded in matter.

2. Telestiké (Statue Animation)
This is the art of consecrating statues to make them houses for divine consciousness.

The theurgist would create a statue using the correct synthemata (hollowed out and filled with specific herbs, gems, or written formulas).

Through ritual, the deity is invited to descend into the statue.

Tantric Parallel: This is almost identical to the Hindu practice of Prana Pratishtha (breathing life into an idol), turning a stone sculpture into a Murti (living embodiment).

3. The Role of the Operator
In low magic (goetia), the wizard tries to command spirits to do their bidding (get money, find love). In theurgy, the operator submits to the ritual to be transformed by it. The goal is not to bring the god down to serve you, but to use the ritual to lift yourself up to the god.

The Three Levels of Theurgy
Iamblichus categorized theurgy into a hierarchy of practice, which mirrors the progression from external ritual to internal meditation:

Material Theurgy: Using physical objects (stones, herbs, animal sacrifices). This was for souls heavily bound to the material world who needed tangible props to focus.

Intermediate Theurgy: Using names (onomata barbara or "barbarous names" that have no translatable meaning but pure vibrational power) and mental commands. This bridges the gap between matter and mind.

Noetic Theurgy: Purely mental or contemplative ritual. At this stage, the theurgist transcends symbols and unites directly with the divine forms.

Why It Resembles Tantra
Ritual is Essential: Both systems agree that philosophy is not enough; you must do something.

Sacred Materiality: Both view the material world as containing the "seeds" of the divine.

The "Vehicle" of the Soul: Theurgy posits that the soul has a "pneumatic vehicle" (a subtle body/astral body) called the ochema. Ritual purifies this subtle body, allowing it to ascend. This is parallel to the purification of the nadis and chakras in Tantra.

How to Explore This Further

If you want to read the primary source manual on this:

"On the Mysteries" (De Mysteriis) by Iamblichus: This is the defense of ritual magic against the intellectual critics of his time. It is dense but foundational.

"The Chaldean Oracles": A fragmented mystical text from the 2nd century that Iamblichus treated as sacred scripture. It contains cryptic verses about the fire of the soul and the structure of the cosmos.

Theurgy is essentially the "high magic" of the West—the practice where the ritual robe and the philosopher's cloak become the same garment.
